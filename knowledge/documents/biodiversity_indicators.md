# Biodiversity Indicators: Species Richness and Habitat Diversity

Species richness (the count of distinct species present) and habitat diversity
(the variety of structural niches available — canopy layers, soil types, moisture
gradients) are the two most commonly used proxy indicators for ecosystem health,
because they are far easier to measure repeatedly than total ecosystem function.

Pollinator diversity in particular is a sensitive early-warning indicator: pollinators
respond quickly (within a single season) to changes in floral diversity, pesticide
load, and habitat connectivity, making them a useful leading signal before slower
indicators (soil microbial diversity, bird populations) show measurable change.

Soil microbial and fungal diversity underpins above-ground biodiversity indirectly:
mycorrhizal fungal networks improve plant nutrient uptake and drought resilience,
and their diversity correlates strongly with soil organic carbon and reduced tillage
intensity. Declines in soil biodiversity often precede visible above-ground
biodiversity loss by one or more growing seasons, meaning soil metrics are a leading
indicator worth monitoring even when above-ground conditions still look stable.

The Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD) and IPBES (Intergovernmental
Science-Policy Platform on Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services) assessments
consistently identify land-use change and habitat fragmentation as the largest
drivers of terrestrial biodiversity loss globally, ahead of climate change and
pollution individually — though the three factors compound each other rather than
acting independently.
