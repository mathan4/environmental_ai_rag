# Soil Health: Organic Carbon, pH, and Moisture

Soil organic carbon (SOC) is one of the strongest single predictors of below-ground
biodiversity. Soils below roughly 0.5% SOC typically support sparse microbial and
fungal communities, weak nutrient cycling, and poor water-holding capacity, which in
turn limits plant diversity above ground. Semi-arid croplands under continuous
monoculture frequently fall into this range because residue removal and tillage both
accelerate carbon loss.

Legume-based cover cropping and intercropping are among the most consistently
effective interventions for rebuilding SOC. Nitrogen-fixing legumes add organic matter
through root turnover and biomass incorporation, and organizations including the Food
and Agriculture Organization (FAO) have documented soil organic carbon gains in the
range of roughly 15-25% over a 2-3 year window under sustained cover-cropping
regimes, with the effect being strongest in degraded or low-carbon starting soils.
Agroforestry systems (integrating trees with crops or pasture) produce similar or
larger gains over longer time horizons (5-10 years) because tree root systems and leaf
litter add a deeper, more persistent carbon pool than annual cover crops alone.

Soil pH strongly mediates nutrient availability: most temperate and tropical crop and
native plant species tolerate a pH range of about 6.0-7.5; outside that range,
micronutrient lockup (e.g. iron, zinc at high pH; aluminum toxicity at low pH)
suppresses both crop yield and understory plant diversity, which cascades to reduced
insect and pollinator diversity.

Soil moisture retention is directly coupled to SOC: each 1% increase in soil organic
matter is commonly associated with meaningfully increased water-holding capacity per
unit volume of soil (widely cited agronomic estimates), which matters disproportionately
in low-rainfall and semi-arid regions where moisture, not nutrients, is the limiting
factor for both crop and wild plant survival.
