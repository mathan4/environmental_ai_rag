# Human Impact: Pollution and Deforestation

Agrochemical pollution (synthetic pesticide and herbicide runoff) is the human-impact
factor most tightly linked to pollinator and aquatic biodiversity decline
specifically, because both groups have narrow tolerance windows and are directly
exposed via foraging or waterway contact. Reduced-input or integrated pest management
approaches, paired with habitat interventions (hedgerows, buffer strips), are commonly
cited as more durable than input reduction alone, because the habitat provides refuge
that allows pollinator and predator-insect populations to recover between exposure
events.

Deforestation and clearing for cropland expansion is the largest single contributor to
terrestrial habitat loss globally per CBD and IPBES assessments, and its biodiversity
impact is disproportionate to the land area cleared because forest edges create
fragmentation effects extending well beyond the cleared boundary (edge effects can
alter microclimate and species composition tens to over a hundred meters into
adjacent remaining forest, depending on forest type).

Riparian (waterway-adjacent) deforestation carries a compounding penalty: it removes
both terrestrial habitat and the vegetative buffer that filters agricultural runoff
before it reaches waterways, so riparian buffer restoration is frequently identified as
a disproportionately high-value intervention relative to its land-area cost — it
simultaneously improves water quality, aquatic biodiversity, and terrestrial
corridor connectivity.
