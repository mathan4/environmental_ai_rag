# Climate Factors: Temperature and Rainfall

Rainfall variability, more than total annual rainfall, is often the dominant climate
stressor for biodiversity in semi-arid and arid regions. Species (both crop and wild)
adapted to a region's historical rainfall pattern can tolerate a "low but predictable"
regime better than an equally dry but erratic one, because erratic rainfall disrupts
flowering and breeding timing across many species simultaneously.

IPCC Working Group II assessments identify increasing rainfall variability and
lengthening dry spells as a primary compounding factor in semi-arid land degradation,
particularly where it interacts with existing low soil organic carbon: dry soils with
low SOC lose what little water-holding capacity they have fastest, creating a
feedback loop where each dry spell degrades the soil's ability to buffer the next one.

Temperature increases interact with land cover: bare or sparsely vegetated soil
(common in monoculture between growing seasons) can reach surface temperatures
significantly higher than shaded or cover-cropped soil, which directly stresses soil
microbial communities and can suppress germination of native understory species even
when moisture is otherwise adequate. This is one of the mechanisms by which land-use
changes (tree cover, cover cropping) produce a climate-buffering effect independent of
their soil-carbon or habitat benefits.
